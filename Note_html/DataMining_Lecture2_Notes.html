Three Overlapping Cousins

Before diving into techniques, it's worth pausing on a bit of terminology hygiene. Three terms get thrown around constantly, often interchangeably, even though they are not the same thing: Data Mining, Machine Learning (ML), and Statistics. There is a lot of genuine overlap between them, and people will use them loosely in conversation — but it helps to know what each one is fundamentally about before we start blurring the lines ourselves.

The cleanest way to separate them is to start with the most mathematically well-defined of the three: Statistics.

  • Statistics is fundamentally about probability distributions and everything derived from them — P(x), P(y∣x), conditional probability, Bayes' rule, and so on. If a question can be phrased as "model the process that generated this data," you're in statistics territory.
  • Machine Learning is, at a high level, function approximation. You are trying to learn some function fθ​ that maps inputs to outputs, i.e. fθ​:x→y. It's not really about why the data looks the way it does — it's about finding the function that minimizes prediction error.
  • Data Mining doesn't come with its own clean mathematical machinery — it's a much broader, more descriptive term. But by definition, it always starts from a dataset (call it D), and the central question is: what useful structure or pattern exists in D?

None of these are truly independent. Machine learning uses datasets (D) for training, data mining leans on statistical measures to validate what it finds, and statistics underlies both. Think of it less as three isolated boxes and more as three different lenses pointed at data.

Breaking each discipline down further into its "core ingredients":

Discipline Core Question Typical Outputs
Data Mining What useful structure or pattern exists in D? Association rules, clusters, summaries
Machine Learning What function f minimizes the prediction error? Predictors, classifiers
Statistics How is the data actually generated (modeling the data-generation process)? Parameter estimates, inferences

Note for Review: Don't lose sleep memorizing this table. The instructor is explicit that this isn't something you'll be quizzed on verbatim — the real point is to develop the instinct to ask *"which flavor of question am I actually asking?"* before you pick a technique. The same dataset can support a statistics question, an ML question, and a data mining question simultaneously.

Concretely, this leads to the following associations:

  • Data Mining → structure/patterns → association rules, clustering, summaries.
  • Machine Learning → minimizing prediction error → designing a predictor or classifier.
  • Statistics → modeling the data-generation process → parameter estimation and inference.

The Statistics Angle: Mean Marks and the Sample-Population Trap

Here's a deceptively simple question, the kind that predates both machine learning and data mining as named disciplines:

How are the mean marks distributed?

Imagine you have several courses, and for each course you compute the class mean:

  • Course C1​: mean =57.9
  • Course C2​: mean =65.74
  • Course C3​: mean =47.49
  • … and so on for every course.

You could ask how these means are distributed relative to one another, or how an individual student's average sits relative to their peers. This looks harmless, but there's a catch hiding in plain sight: the question never specifies which students. Mean marks of whom? Online BSc students only? On-campus students? Students from other universities entirely? The question, as posed, is wide open.

This is exactly where the concept of sample vs. population enters. You typically only have access to a sample — say, one batch of students — but the goal is usually to draw a conclusion about the population at large (students in general). That leap from sample to population is called inference (or inferential modeling), and it is one of the oldest and most central ideas in statistics.

Note for Review: Whenever a question sounds like "what is the average/typical value of X?", your first instinct should be to ask *"average over which population, and is my sample actually representative of it?"* This single habit prevents a huge fraction of bad conclusions in data analysis.

The Data Mining Angle: Patterns, Rules, Clusters, and Anomalies

Now consider the same flavor of exercise from the data mining side. Suppose you have a dataset D consisting of n data points (possibly large vectors):

D={xi​}i=1n​

The goal of data mining is to extract useful patterns from D. This typically shows up in a few recurring forms:

1. Association Rules — relating one object or event to another, e.g., A→B. The classic example is market-basket analysis: if customers who buy product A also tend to buy product B, that association can directly inform sales strategy (e.g., bundling or shelf placement).

2. Clustering — grouping the observations in D into subsets that share internal characteristics:

D=C1​∪C2​∪C3​

Even if you have thousands of raw data points, clustering might reveal that they really fall into just a handful of distinct groups, each with its own story (e.g., three different customer behavior patterns hiding inside one month of sales data). Note that clustering is fundamentally an unsupervised task — there are no pre-assigned labels; the structure has to emerge from the data itself.

3. Anomaly Detection — flagging observations that are unusual relative to the rest of the data (e.g., a sudden, unexplained spike in sales for one product). On its own, "anomaly detection" is just an English phrase — it carries no mathematical content until it's pinned down. A more rigorous formulation looks like this:

Find xi​ whose probability is low, or whose distance to other data points is unusually large or small relative to the rest of the dataset.

brucks

This is the crucial move: probability can be defined precisely, distance can be defined precisely (Euclidean distance, etc.), and "large/small" can be operationalized with a threshold (e.g., flag anything with distance below 0.1 as anomalous). Only once "anomaly" is translated into probability and distance does it become something you can actually compute.

Statistics as the Foundation

This naturally leads to a broader point worth internalizing:

Statistics is the foundation of both machine learning and data mining — and of plenty of other fields too, from communication systems to signal processing to robotics. Anywhere you need to quantify uncertainty or structure, statistics is doing the heavy lifting underneath.

Practically, this means: if your statistics fundamentals feel shaky, now is the ideal time to shore them up, since every advanced technique in this course eventually falls back on probability and distributions.

Worked Example: The Hospital with a Million Records

Consider a hospital with roughly 1 million patient records — call this dataset D. The exact same dataset can support three structurally different kinds of questions, one from each discipline.

Question 1 (Statistics): Is blood pressure associated with stroke risk?

At face value, this sounds like a plain yes/no question. But mathematically, it should be translated into estimating a conditional probability:

P(Stroke∣BP)

and this in turn gets addressed with hypothesis testing, which we'll cover briefly later in the course. Crucially, this question is generic — it isn't asking "does this particular dataset show BP linked to stroke," it's asking whether the relationship holds beyond this one sample, i.e., a claim about the broader patient population (even though 1 million patients is nowhere close to "everyone"). Any honest answer will come with some associated uncertainty rather than a flat "yes" or "no."

Question 2 (Machine Learning): Given a patient's features, will they have a stroke?

Here the task shifts. Using the dataset D, the patient features (BP, age, illness history, treatment, medication, etc.) are used to train a model — a predictor or classifier — so that given a new patient (one not in D), the model outputs a prediction:

f(x)→{stroke,no stroke}

This is a binary classification problem. The key word here is generalization: the whole point is to say something useful about a patient who is not already in the training data (if the patient were already in D, there'd be nothing to predict — you'd already know the outcome). Generalization is really just inference wearing an ML hat — under the hood, you're still leaning heavily on statistics.

Question 3 (Data Mining): What unexpected or new pattern emerges from the data?

This is the widest and vaguest of the three: no mention of stroke, BP, age, or any specific variable. Just: look at the data, look at correlations across features, and tell me if anything interesting or unexpected shows up. This falls squarely under knowledge discovery, and it is naturally viewed from an unsupervised perspective (as opposed to ML, which — in this framing — is more supervised, though ML absolutely can be unsupervised too; the boundaries aren't rigid).

The takeaway: all three questions rely on probability and statistics somewhere under the hood, but they differ enormously in scope — from a narrow, specific hypothesis (Q1), to a feature-driven prediction task (Q2), to a fully open-ended exploratory search (Q3).

Worked Example: The Streaming Service

Consider a streaming platform (think Netflix, Spotify, Amazon-style services) and viewers who either watched a science documentary or didn't, and separately, either watched an AI documentary or didn't. The raw counts look like this:

Watched AI documentary Did not watch AI documentary Total
Watched Science documentary 300 700 1000
Did not watch Science documentary 1200 3800 5000
Total 1500 4500 6000

From this contingency table, two simple percentages fall out immediately:

  • Among science-documentary viewers, the fraction who also watched an AI documentary: 1000300​=30%
  • Among viewers who did not watch a science documentary, the fraction who still watched an AI documentary: 50001200​=24%

Computing those two percentages is a genuinely trivial exercise in data mining — it's literally just division. And this is exactly where a common mistake creeps in: stopping here. Reporting "30% did X, 24% did Y" is a number, but on its own it isn't very informative. The far more interesting follow-up question is:

Is the observed pattern (30% vs. 24%) random, or systematic?

"Random" and "systematic" sound like everyday English words, but in this context they have precise mathematical meanings rooted in probability. This is where you'd bring in a statistical (hypothesis) test: you treat "watching the science documentary" as the potential cause, and "watching (or not watching) the AI documentary" as the effect, and you use a test statistic and its distribution to judge whether the 6-point gap (30% vs. 24%) is big enough to reflect a genuine relationship, or small enough to plausibly be noise.

Note for Review: This is a great mini-template for real-world data mining work: (1) extract the simple descriptive pattern first (percentages, counts, means — the easy part), then (2) test whether that pattern is statistically meaningful before you act on it. Skipping step 2 is how "insights" decks end up full of noise dressed up as signal.

Four Fundamental Questions About Data

Stepping back from specific examples, before applying any data mining technique to a dataset, there are four questions worth asking about the data itself. These aren't deeply conceptual on their own — they're mostly matters of definition and discipline — but they recur constantly and will structure much of the course going forward.

  1. What are the attributes or features of the data?
  2. How do you summarize the data? (The way you choose to summarize directly shapes what conclusions you can later draw — this is not a neutral step.)
  3. Is the raw data appropriate for data mining? (Do you need preprocessing — handling noise, missing values, cleaning — before applying any technique at all?)
  4. Is the data telling a story or conclusion beyond itself? (i.e., can you generalize beyond the specific dataset you have?)

Question 4 is by far the hardest, and it is the one most directly tied to generalization — the same idea that showed up earlier with the hospital's ML classifier predicting stroke risk for a new patient. Questions 1–3 are relatively independent of each other, but Question 4 depends on all of them: how you define attributes, how you summarize, and how (or whether) you clean the data will all shape whether any conclusion you draw actually holds up beyond the dataset in front of you.

These four questions won't be fully answered in a single lecture — they form the backbone of the discussions to come across the next several lectures, and it's worth keeping them in mind (and revisiting/refining them) as the course progresses.

Think About It

A few questions to chew on before the next lecture — these aren't recall questions, they're meant to make you reason a bit:

  1. Suppose a retailer finds that "customers who buy sunscreen also buy sunglasses" (an association rule, A → B) with high confidence. Why might this rule be far less "actionable" for a business decision than it first appears, and what statistical check would you want to run before trusting it enough to act on it (e.g., changing store layout or promotions)?

  2. The hospital example split one dataset into three questions of increasing "width" (BP-stroke association → per-patient stroke prediction → open-ended pattern discovery). If you only had resources to pursue one of these three lines of analysis for a hospital trying to reduce stroke incidents, which would you prioritize, and what would you be giving up by not pursuing the other two?

  3. In the streaming service example, a 30% vs. 24% gap was called out as needing a test for "random vs. systematic." Construct a scenario where a much larger percentage gap (say 60% vs. 10%) could still plausibly be "random noise," and a scenario where a much smaller gap (say 31% vs. 30%) could still be strongly systematic. What property of the data (beyond the raw percentages) is doing the real work in each case?

  4. The lecture argues that "anomaly detection" is meaningless without translating it into probability or distance. Pick a real-world anomaly detection scenario (e.g., credit card fraud, a factory sensor reading, a sudden traffic spike on a website) and propose two different mathematical formalizations of "anomaly" for it. Would the two formalizations flag the same events as anomalous, or could they disagree — and why?

  5. Question 4 ("Is the data telling a story beyond itself?") was called the hardest of the four fundamental questions, and it was said to depend on Questions 1–3. Construct a concrete example where a poor choice made in Question 2 (how you summarize the data) would cause you to draw a conclusion in Question 4 that does not actually generalize beyond your specific dataset — i.e., show how a summarization choice can quietly manufacture a false "story."
